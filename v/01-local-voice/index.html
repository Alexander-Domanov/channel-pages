A voice for your videos, free

I wanted to know whether my laptop could narrate my videos instead of a monthly voice subscription. It can. This page is the whole experiment.

Tested on: a Mac with an M2 chip and 8 GB of memory.

Both programs run on Windows and Linux too. I didn't test those, and the setup page marks which lines come from the projects' own instructions rather than from me.

What I used How it went Try it yourself What matters What it costs The catch

What I used

  • OmniVoice — the fast one. Open source, 646 languages. github.com/k2-fsa/OmniVoice
  • Qwen3-TTS — the second voice, to compare against. Open source, ten languages. github.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-TTS
  • Python 3.12, with each program in its own folder.
  • Ten seconds of my own voice, recorded once.

Both run on the laptop. Nothing is sent anywhere, and neither needs a graphics card.

Both licences allow commercial use. If you plan to sell something built on top of the model, read the licence once yourself.

How it went

The same twenty-two seconds of speech, given to both programs on the same machine:

Program22 seconds of speechA 20-minute narration
OmniVoice26 secondsabout 23 minutes
Qwen3-TTS73 secondsabout an hour

Memory was never the problem: peak use stayed under 2 GB while it worked.

OmniVoice is the one to start with. Twenty minutes of narration takes it about twenty-three minutes, which means the next script gets written while it talks. Keep Qwen3-TTS for a second voice, to compare a line that sounds off.

How long you'll wait — both programs timed on the same machine.

Try it yourself

Python 3.12 or newer, then these two lines.

The one step people skip: each program goes in its own folder. In one folder they break each other.

pip install torch==2.8.0 torchaudio==2.8.0
pip install omnivoice

The first run downloads the model — a few gigabytes, once. Then hand the program your text and your sample:

omnivoice-infer --text "..." --ref_audio my_voice.wav --output mine.wav

Prefer clicking to typing: omnivoice-demo --port 8001 opens the same program in your browser.

Every command, what you should see after it, and what to do when it won't start: Setting it up. The recording it copies: ten seconds of your voice.

What matters

Four things decided how good this sounds, and only one of them is the software.

  • The sample does most of the work. Ten seconds, read at the pace you want to be listened to. Record it in the language you want the voice to sound native in — a Russian sample carries a Russian accent into English and Spanish.
  • Keep the recording. When a program updates, the voice gets rebuilt from that file. Nothing else remembers it.
  • Phone memos are the wrong format. They come out as .m4a and the program wants .wav; the sample page has three ways to fix that.
  • The two programs need two folders. This is the trap that costs an evening, and the setup page walks through it line by line.

Where the numbers come from. ElevenLabs, Descript and Speechify come from their own pricing pages, read on October 5, 2026. The narrator rate is a studio price list at voicecrafters.com.

The language count is OmniVoice's own figure. Prices move, and first months are often discounted — check before you pay anyone.

$0 means no subscription. Your time and the electricity aren't in the table.

The catch

It sounds good. Not "good for a robot", just good — and it still isn't a person. For narration on a channel like this one, that's enough for me. For a brand's ad, I would hire a narrator.

Your own clone is fine by YouTube's rules. Someone else's voice needs their permission first, and a disclosure on the video.

The sample page has the short version, YouTube's own page has the rules, and they do change.

Verdict: if the laptop is already on your desk, there's little reason to pay for every minute of narration — unless you want the convenience of a cloud service. I didn't.
